First Meeting with a New Leader Interview Questions

How to handle a first meeting with a new leader: introducing yourself, your scope and your value. Each question has why it comes up, a sample answer and a tip. The sample answers use an example Senior ERP Analyst; swap in your own projects, wins and goals. Tap a question to see the answer.

Questions and answers by Hari Pokkuluri

Senior Oracle ERP Solution Architect with 22+ years in Oracle Fusion Cloud and E-Business Suite: Financials, SCM, HCM and Procurement. Oracle-certified in R11/R12 Financials and OCI AI Foundations. Arahasoft.

1Tell me about yourself and your role.IntroductionBasicTop question

Why they ask: a new leader is building a mental map of the organization. You have about 60 seconds to be memorable.

Sample answer:

"I'm Senior ERP Analyst on the ERP applications team. I own the finance applications and the integrations that feed them. Right now my main focus is the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project. A recent result I'm proud of is cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule. Outside of the day-to-day, I'm looking to grow toward leading a larger team within the next year."

Tip: Role, scope, current focus, one win — then stop and let them ask.

Likely follow-up

How long should that introduction be?

About a minute: role, scope, one recent result and what you're focused on next — for example 'I'm Senior ERP Analyst on the ERP applications team; we support 900 finance users; last quarter we cut close by two days; next is the Redwood rollout.' Then ask about their priorities.

2What does your team do, in one sentence?IntroductionBasicTop question

Why they ask: they'll repeat your description to others.

Sample answer:

"We keep the business systems running and evolving — the finance applications and the integrations that feed them — so operations, finance and customers can work without disruption."

Tip: Describe the business value, not the technology.

Likely follow-up

What if my team does many things?

Pick the outcome that matters most to the business and lead with it — 'We keep finance running on time: close, payments and reporting.' You can add the details later; a new leader needs the headline first.

3What value does your area bring to the business?ValueBasicTop question

Why they ask: new leaders decide where to invest and what to cut.

Sample answer:

"Three things: stability (critical processes run every day), speed (we deliver changes the business needs, like the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project), and risk reduction (audits and controls). A concrete example is cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule."

Tip: Use business language — money, risk, speed, customers.

Likely follow-up

How do I make value concrete?

Use one number — like 'we support a $2 billion close in five days' or 'we saved $800,000 last year through automation'. A concrete figure tells a new leader why your area matters.

4What are your top priorities right now?PrioritiesBasicTop question

Why they ask: to see if your priorities match theirs.

Sample answer:

"First, the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project. Second, keeping operations stable during the change. Third, building team depth so we're not dependent on one or two people. I'd like to hear your priorities so we can align."

Tip: Share three and invite their view.

Likely follow-up

What if my priorities conflict with theirs?

Ask how they see the priorities and agree which should change — 'Here's what we're working on; does that match what you need? What would you move up or down?' Aligning early prevents months of wasted effort.

5What should I know that I might not hear from others?ListeningIn-depth

Why they ask: they want honest insight without politics.

Sample answer:

"Our team is strong, but we carry a lot of single points of knowledge, which is a risk. Also, the business values responsiveness highly — a tight go-live date and one open role on the team is the area where they feel the most pain right now."

Tip: Be candid about systems and risks. Don't criticize individuals.

Likely follow-up

How candid should I be with a new leader?

Candid about facts and risks, never gossip about people.

6What are the biggest challenges you face?ChallengesBasicTop question

Why they ask: to understand risks and where they can help.

Sample answer:

"The main challenge is a tight go-live date and one open role on the team. We're managing it through clear priorities and phased delivery. Where your support would help most is in aligning business stakeholders on what comes first."

Tip: Name the challenge, your plan, and where they can help.

Likely follow-up

Should I bring solutions with challenges?

Yes, at least one option for each challenge you raise — 'Our biggest challenge is testing time for quarterly updates; automating the top 50 tests would cut it in half.' It shows you're part of the solution.

7What questions should I ask a new leader?Their PrioritiesBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) good questions build the relationship.

Sample answer (questions to ask):

  • "What are your top priorities for the first 90 days?"
  • "What does success look like for our area in a year?"
  • "How do you like to receive updates and escalations?"
  • "What would you like me to keep doing, and what should change?"

Tip: Listen more than you talk in this first meeting.

Likely follow-up

What's the best question to ask?

What does success look like for you in the first six months?

8What do you think my priorities should be?Their PrioritiesIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a new leader may test your perspective on the business.

Sample answer:

"From where I sit, I'd focus on three things: getting the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project over the line, reducing operational risk, and clarifying how teams prioritize requests. I'm curious how that compares with what you've heard so far."

Tip: Offer a view tied to business outcomes, humbly.

Likely follow-up

What if I don't know what they expect?

Share your view, then ask what they'd change — 'I think we should focus on close automation and audit readiness. What would you add or change?' A new leader appreciates a starting point more than a blank page.

9How do you like to work?Working StyleBasicTop question

Why they ask: setting up the relationship.

Sample answer:

"I like clear priorities and room to execute. I send a short weekly update and raise risks early with options. I value direct feedback. How do you prefer to work and communicate?"

Tip: Share briefly, then adapt to them.

Likely follow-up

What should I learn about their style?

How they like updates — email, chat or meetings — how often, how fast they decide, how much detail they want and when to escalate. Ask directly: 'How do you like to be kept informed?'

10How often should we meet?Working StyleBasic

Why they ask: to establish cadence.

Sample answer:

"A 30-minute 1:1 every two weeks would work well, plus a short written update weekly. If you prefer more frequent check-ins during your first months, I'm happy to do weekly."

Tip: Suggest a cadence and stay flexible.

Likely follow-up

What cadence works with a new leader?

Weekly or every two weeks at first, then adjust.

11Tell me about your team.TeamIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: talent assessment and where to invest.

Sample answer:

"We have strong depth in our core areas, and two people who are ready for more responsibility. Our gaps are in newer technologies and backup coverage. Morale is good, especially after cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule."

Tip: Highlight strengths and stars; be constructive about gaps.

Likely follow-up

How do I talk about weaker team members?

Focus on strengths and development plans, not labels — 'Priya is our strongest on integrations; Mark is building his reporting skills and improving fast.' First impressions of the team stick, so be fair and specific.

12What are you most proud of recently?WinsBasic

Why they ask: a quick read on your impact.

Sample answer:

"cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule. What made it work was early alignment with the business and disciplined testing. It reduced issues and freed the business team to focus on customers."

Tip: One win with business impact and what made it work.

Likely follow-up

How do I avoid sounding boastful?

Credit the team and state the business impact.

13What isn't working that I should fix?ProblemsIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: new leaders want quick wins.

Sample answer:

"Request intake and prioritization across teams. Today urgent requests interrupt planned work, so delivery feels unpredictable. A single intake with clear priority rules would help everyone."

Tip: Suggest systemic fixes, not complaints about people.

Likely follow-up

How do I raise problems without blaming my predecessor?

Describe the problem and the fix, not who caused it — 'Our change process takes too long for small fixes; a lighter path would help.' Blaming the previous leader makes you look political, even if it's true.

14How did things work under my predecessor?Previous LeaderIn-depth

Why they ask: context, but it can be a trap.

Sample answer:

"We had clear quarterly priorities and regular reviews, which worked well. One thing we could improve is faster decisions on cross-team trade-offs. I'm open to whatever approach you prefer."

Tip: Stay positive and factual. Never criticize the previous leader.

Likely follow-up

What if things worked badly before?

Be factual and forward-looking.

15What are your career goals?CareerBasic

Why they ask: succession planning and engagement.

Sample answer:

"I'm aiming for leading a larger team within the next year. I'm excited to learn from your experience, and I'd value your view on what I'd need to demonstrate to get there."

Tip: Share goals briefly; don't make the first meeting about promotion.

Likely follow-up

How open should I be about career goals?

Clear about direction, flexible about timing.

16If you had my job, what would you do first?IdeasIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: to hear ideas and assess strategic thinking.

Sample answer:

"I'd spend the first weeks listening to key stakeholders, then pick two quick wins — probably clearer prioritization and visible progress on the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project — while building a longer-term roadmap tied to a reliable close, fewer manual steps and faster delivery for finance."

Tip: Balance listening with action-oriented ideas.

Likely follow-up

What's a good answer?

Listen first, then focus on the one or two issues with the biggest impact — for example 'I'd spend a few weeks with the teams and users, then fix the testing bottleneck, because it slows everything else.'

17Who are the key people I should meet?StakeholdersBasic

Why they ask: you can help them navigate.

Sample answer:

"I'd suggest the heads of the main business teams we support, our key vendor partner, and the people who run critical operations day to day. Happy to set up introductions."

Tip: Offer introductions — it builds goodwill.

Likely follow-up

How do I suggest people without overstepping?

Explain why each person matters to their goals.

18What could go wrong in the next six months?RisksIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: risk awareness.

Sample answer:

"The biggest risk is a tight go-live date and one open role on the team colliding with year-end activity. Second is losing key knowledge if someone leaves. We're mitigating with a phased plan and cross-training."

Tip: Name risks with mitigation.

Likely follow-up

How do I raise risks without sounding negative?

Pair each risk with a mitigation — 'The vendor contract renews in March; we're preparing a comparison so you have options.' That makes you sound prepared rather than negative.

19How should I follow up after the first meeting?Follow-upBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) follow-up shows reliability.

Sample answer (what to send):

"Thank you for the time today. As discussed: I'll send a one-page overview of our scope and current priorities by Friday, and set up introductions with the business leads. Looking forward to working together."

Tip: Send within 24 hours with concrete next steps.

Likely follow-up

What should the follow-up include?

A thank-you, a short summary and any actions I agreed.

20What should my one-page overview for a new leader include?One-pagerBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) a crisp summary is valuable to them.

Sample answer (contents):

  • Scope: the finance applications and the integrations that feed them.
  • Team structure and key people.
  • Current priorities: the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project.
  • Recent wins: cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule.
  • Top risks and asks: a tight go-live date and one open role on the team.
  • Key metrics.

Tip: Keep it to one page with facts and numbers.

Likely follow-up

How long should the overview be?

One page: scope, priorities, metrics, risks and team.

21Are you open to changes in how we work?ChangeBasic

Why they ask: new leaders often bring changes.

Sample answer:

"Absolutely. I'd appreciate understanding the goals behind any changes so I can help the team adopt them. I'll also share what's worked well so we keep the strengths."

Tip: Be open and constructive.

Likely follow-up

What if I disagree with a change?

Raise concerns with data, then commit if they decide.

22Do you have any concerns about the leadership change?ConcernsIn-depth

Why they ask: to surface worries early.

Sample answer:

"My main interest is continuity on the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project and clarity on decision-making during the transition. Beyond that, I'm looking forward to it."

Tip: Raise practical concerns, not personal anxiety.

Likely follow-up

Should I share concerns honestly?

Yes, constructively, and say how I'll help.

23How do you measure success in your area?MetricsBasic

Why they ask: data-driven leaders want metrics.

Sample answer:

"We track service stability (incidents and resolution times), delivery against plan, stakeholder satisfaction and audit findings. Recent trends are positive — for example cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule."

Tip: Bring three or four metrics and trends.

Likely follow-up

What if we don't track metrics?

Propose a few simple ones and how we'd measure them.

24What resources does your area have?BudgetIn-depth

Why they ask: understanding costs and constraints.

Sample answer:

"We have our internal team plus a support partner for routine work. Spending is mostly people and licenses. The constraint is capacity during peaks like the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project."

Tip: Be factual and highlight constraints.

Likely follow-up

What if resources are tight?

Show the trade-offs and what more resources would unlock.

25What do you need from me?ExpectationsBasic

Why they ask: a partnership mindset.

Sample answer:

"Clarity on priorities, quick decisions on cross-team trade-offs, and support with stakeholders when we need to say 'not now'. In return, you'll get transparent updates and no surprises."

Tip: Ask for a few clear things and offer something in return.

Likely follow-up

What's a reasonable ask?

Clear priorities and quick decisions on blockers.

26How do I make a strong first impression?ToneBasicTop question

Why they ask: (your own question) first impressions stick.

Sample answer (approach):

  • Come prepared with your one-pager.
  • Be concise and positive.
  • Show you understand the business, not just your tasks.
  • Ask good questions and listen.
  • Follow up quickly.

Tip: Confidence plus curiosity.

Likely follow-up

What makes the biggest impression?

Preparation and a clear, concise picture of your area — one page with scope, priorities, results and risks — plus a couple of good questions about their goals. Being organized and brief stands out.

27What if the new leader asks about a difficult colleague?PoliticsIn-depth

Why they ask: a test of professionalism.

Sample answer:

"We've had different views on priorities at times, which is natural with competing demands. We've found ways to work through it, and clearer prioritization rules would help both teams."

Tip: Stay factual and constructive; avoid gossip.

Likely follow-up

What if I'm asked directly?

Stay factual and professional, focus on work impact.

28Will my role change under the new leader?ReorganizationIn-depth

Why they ask: (your own question) uncertainty is common after leadership changes.

Sample answer (what to say):

"I'm excited to support your priorities. If you're considering changes to structure or roles, I'd value hearing early, and I'm keen to contribute where I can add the most value — especially around the finance applications and the integrations that feed them."

Tip: Show flexibility and highlight your value.

Likely follow-up

How do I ask without sounding anxious?

Ask how they see the role evolving and how I can contribute.

29What quick wins can you deliver?Quick WinsBasicTop question

Why they ask: new leaders want early momentum.

Sample answer:

"In the next 30 days: finish the next milestone of the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project, publish a simple dashboard of our key metrics, and clean up the request backlog with business owners."

Tip: Offer specific, achievable 30-day wins.

Likely follow-up

What makes a good quick win?

Visible, low-risk and done within weeks — like fixing a report the leader's team complains about, or clearing a backlog of small requests. Quick wins build credibility for the bigger changes.

30Where should this area be in two years?Long-termIn-depth

Why they ask: strategic thinking.

Sample answer:

"Highly automated operations, a modern platform that supports a reliable close, fewer manual steps and faster delivery for finance, strong business partnership, and a team with depth and growth paths."

Tip: Paint a clear, business-aligned picture.

Likely follow-up

How do I share a long-term view?

Link it to business goals and outline steps.

31What do our business customers say about your team?CustomerBasic

Why they ask: reputation check.

Sample answer:

"They value our responsiveness and expertise, especially after cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule. The main feedback is wanting more visibility on when requests will be done, which we're improving with a shared roadmap."

Tip: Share praise and constructive feedback.

Likely follow-up

What if feedback is mixed?

Share it honestly with what we're doing about it.

32What's something people don't know about you?PersonalBasic

Why they ask: building rapport.

Sample answer:

"Outside work I enjoy cricket and cooking for family gatherings. And professionally, I've worked across both business and technical roles, which helps me bridge the two."

Tip: Something light and genuine, plus a professional angle.

Likely follow-up

Why does this question come up?

They're building a relationship; share something light and genuine.

33When should I bring things to you?EscalationBasic

Why they ask: setting decision rights early.

Sample answer:

"I'll handle day-to-day decisions within my scope. I'll bring you risks to commitments, cross-team conflicts, customer-impacting issues and anything needing budget. Tell me if you want to adjust that."

Tip: Propose clear rules.

Likely follow-up

How do I agree escalation rules?

Ask for thresholds, like risk, cost or customer impact.

34Should I meet my new leader's boss too?Skip-levelIn-depth

Why they ask: (your own question) navigating levels.

Sample answer (approach):

Build the relationship with your direct leader first. If senior leaders invite you, prepare a short summary, stay aligned with your leader's messages, and tell your leader about the conversation.

Tip: Never go around your new leader.

Likely follow-up

How do I meet their boss appropriately?

Only with the new leader's support, never around them.

35How do I earn a new leader's trust quickly?TrustBasicTop question

Why they ask: (your own question) trust is built through reliability.

Sample answer (approach):

Deliver on small commitments quickly, communicate proactively, bring solutions with problems, be honest about risks, and support their priorities publicly.

Tip: Reliability beats brilliance early on.

Likely follow-up

What builds trust fastest?

Delivering on early commitments — doing what you said by when you said, even small things — plus honest updates when something slips. Trust builds fastest through consistency.

36What feedback would you give the organization?FeedbackIn-depth

Why they ask: candid perspective.

Sample answer:

"We're strong at solving urgent problems but less consistent at planning ahead. More predictable prioritization and investment in team depth would make a big difference."

Tip: Constructive and systemic.

Likely follow-up

How do I give feedback about the organization?

Focus on processes and ideas, not people.

37Anything else you'd like me to know?ClosingBasic

Why they ask: a final open door.

Sample answer:

"Just that I'm committed to making your first months smooth. I'll send the overview and introductions this week, and I'm here if you need context on anything."

Tip: End with support and next steps.

Likely follow-up

What's a good final message?

Your commitment to helping them succeed.

38How do I connect with a new leader I only see on video?RemoteBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) remote relationships need intention.

Sample answer (approach):

Camera on, concise written updates, occasional informal check-ins, and meeting in person when possible.

Tip: Be visible in writing.

Likely follow-up

How do I build rapport on video?

Camera on, brief personal connection and regular check-ins.

39What do you value in a leader?ValuesBasic

Why they ask: understanding what motivates you.

Sample answer:

"Clear direction, trust to execute, honest feedback and support when things get tough. I do my best work with a leader who sets priorities and removes obstacles."

Tip: Positive and specific.

Likely follow-up

How do I answer authentically?

Name a real quality with an example from a past leader.

40How can you help me during my transition?TransitionBasic

Why they ask: offers of help are noticed.

Sample answer:

"I can brief you on systems and history, introduce you to stakeholders, and keep key work stable while you get up to speed. Just tell me where you'd like context first."

Tip: Make concrete offers.

Likely follow-up

What help can I offer?

Context, introductions and quick answers about the area.

41What's our biggest opportunity?StrategyIn-depth

Why they ask: strategic insight.

Sample answer:

"Using the platform we're building with the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project to automate manual work and give leaders better data. That directly supports a reliable close, fewer manual steps and faster delivery for finance."

Tip: Tie opportunity to business goals.

Likely follow-up

How do I frame the opportunity?

With the business value and a first step.

42What would make this partnership successful?Wrap-upBasic

Why they ask: aligning on expectations.

Sample answer:

"Regular communication, shared priorities, quick decisions, and mutual honesty — including feedback in both directions."

Tip: Simple and sincere.

Likely follow-up

What makes partnership work?

Clear expectations and regular communication.

43What should I avoid saying in the first meeting?AvoidBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) common mistakes.

Sample answer (avoid):

  • Complaints about colleagues or the previous leader.
  • Immediately asking for promotion or a raise.
  • Long technical details.
  • Overpromising.

Tip: Positive, concise and business-focused.

Likely follow-up

What should I avoid?

Complaints about the predecessor or colleagues.

44How do I learn what my new leader cares about?LearningBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) alignment.

Sample answer (approach):

Ask directly, read their communications and town hall messages, notice what they ask about in meetings, and confirm in 1:1s.

Tip: Listen for repeated themes.

Likely follow-up

How do I learn their priorities?

Ask directly and watch what they spend time on.

45How do I make my team visible to the new leader?VisibilityBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) recognition and support.

Sample answer (approach):

Invite them to a team meeting, share a short wins summary crediting individuals, and offer a demo of key work.

Tip: Credit the team specifically.

Likely follow-up

How do I showcase the team?

Invite team members to present their work.

46What history should I understand about this area?ContextIn-depth

Why they ask: context helps them avoid mistakes.

Sample answer:

"Key history: the platform has evolved over several years, we've completed major upgrades, and the current focus is the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project. Past lessons — involving the business early and phasing big changes — still apply."

Tip: Brief history plus lessons.

Likely follow-up

What history matters?

Past decisions that still shape how things work.

47How do I stay confident if the new leader is very senior?ConfidenceBasic

Why they ask: (your own question) nerves are normal.

Sample answer (approach):

Prepare your one-pager, know your numbers, remember you're the expert on your area, and treat it as a conversation.

Tip: Preparation builds confidence.

Likely follow-up

How do I stay confident?

Prepare well and focus on facts I know best.

48What's a good agenda for the first meeting with a new leader?AgendaBasicTop question

Why they ask: (your own question) structure keeps it useful.

Sample answer (agenda):

  1. Your role and scope in two minutes: the finance applications and the integrations that feed them.
  2. Current priorities and status: the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project.
  3. Risks and asks: a tight go-live date and one open role on the team.
  4. Their priorities and expectations.
  5. How you'll work together and next steps.

Tip: Leave at least half the time for their priorities.

Likely follow-up

What's a good agenda?

Introductions, their priorities and yours, how you'll work together — updates, decisions, escalation — and next steps. Thirty minutes is enough; finish with one concrete follow-up.

49What will you need from me in the first 90 days?ExpectationsBasic

Why they ask: a new leader wants to know your needs early.

Sample answer:

"Early clarity on your top priorities, quick decisions where work is waiting, and your backing with stakeholders on scope for the supplier onboarding upgrade and the month-end close automation project. I'll keep you informed with a weekly summary."

Tip: Keep the asks few and concrete.

Likely follow-up

What will they likely need?

Clear status, risks and quick wins.

50Why should I trust your judgment?CredibilityIn-depth

Why they ask: a direct test of credibility.

Sample answer:

"My track record: cutting invoice processing time by about 30% and closing the last two quarters on schedule, stable operations through major changes, and honest early warnings when things are at risk. I'd rather show you over the next few weeks than ask you to take my word for it."

Tip: Evidence, then deliver.

Likely follow-up

How do I show good judgment?

With examples of past decisions and their results.
