1What does a Senior Solution Architect own on an Oracle Finance program that a lead consultant doesn't?ArchitectureBasicTop question

Why they ask: to see if you understand the step up from lead to architect.

Answer:

"A lead owns a workstream; an architect owns the whole solution. That means end-to-end design across modules, EPM, integrations, data, security and reporting. It means architecture decisions with documented trade-offs and their long-term cost. It means non-functional requirements: performance, volumes, availability, security, auditability and upgradeability. It means the integration and data architecture, including which platform and who owns master data. I run the design authority that reviews workstream designs for consistency. I make sure today's design fits the roadmap: acquisitions, new countries and AI. And I explain options and risk to the CFO and CIO."

Tip: Open with "a lead owns a workstream, an architect owns the whole". It frames everything else.

Likely follow-up

How do you keep authority as an architect without owning workstreams?

Through design governance. Every cross-module decision — chart of accounts, SLA rules, intercompany, integrations — comes to a design authority I chair, and I make the trade-offs visible to the business owners with options and impacts. People follow the architect because decisions are transparent and documented, not because of reporting lines.

2How do you document and govern architecture decisions?ArchitectureBasic

Why they ask: undocumented decisions get reopened and forgotten.

Answer:

"I use Architecture Decision Records: the context, options considered, the decision, consequences, owner and date. A design authority meets weekly, and workstreams bring any decision that affects others. We agree principles up front, like 'adopt standard first', 'one integration platform', 'no direct database access' and who owns which master data. Requirements trace to design, configuration and tests. And decisions are revisited when their assumptions change."

Tip: Give one principle you've used. It shows the governance is real.

Likely follow-up

What makes an ADR useful years later?

It records the options rejected and why, so future teams don't relitigate or undo a decision without understanding its reasons.

3Design the enterprise structure for a company with 40 legal entities in 15 countries, a US parent reporting in USD under US GAAP.Enterprise StructuresIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a senior architecture question. They want clear choices and trade-offs.

Answer:

"For ledgers, I'd create primary ledgers per country and currency, using one global chart of accounts where possible. For each non-USD ledger, I'd add a USD reporting currency if the business needs daily USD visibility; otherwise translation in consolidation is enough. Secondary ledgers only where local GAAP differs materially and must be kept in Oracle; otherwise local adjustments go in a statutory tool. Ledger sets by region make period management easier. Legal entities get balancing segment values; business units go by country or region, with shared services BUs providing AP and AR. FCCS handles group consolidation, eliminations and minority interests. The trade-offs I'd walk the client through are a global vs local chart, the number of ledgers vs close effort, and the translation approach."

Tip: State the trade-offs at the end. Architects are judged on how they explain choices.

Likely follow-up

Would you ever use one ledger for two countries?

Only if the two countries share the chart of accounts, calendar, currency and accounting method, and statutory needs can be met with balancing values — for example two eurozone entities with the same local GAAP. Most of the time each country needs its own ledger for local currency and statutory reporting.

4When would you put multiple legal entities in one ledger and when would you separate them?Enterprise StructuresIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a key design decision in every Fusion project.

Answer:

"Entities can share a ledger when they share the four Cs: chart of accounts, calendar, currency and accounting method. Then each entity gets its own balancing segment values. That means fewer ledgers to close, simpler intercompany and shared reporting. I'd separate them when any of the four Cs differ, or when statutory reporting or local auditors need strict separation, when close timelines differ, when security is easier at ledger level, or when a divestiture is planned and a clean carve-out matters. The trade-off with many entities in one ledger is that intercompany balancing rules and security by balancing segment need careful design."

Tip: Mention the divestiture case. It shows you think beyond the setup screen.

Likely follow-up

What's the downside of too many ledgers?

More period opening and closing, revaluation and translation runs, and more reporting overhead. Ledger sets help by letting you run those processes across many ledgers at once, but each ledger still needs its own reconciliations and security. So I don't create a ledger unless currency, calendar, COA or GAAP truly require it.

5How do you choose between REST APIs, FBDI, SOAP and business events for Finance integrations?IntegrationIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: picking the wrong pattern causes performance and support problems.

Answer:

"REST is for real-time, low-to-medium volume, record-level work, like creating a supplier or checking an invoice status, within rate limits and payload sizes. FBDI is for high-volume batch loads like journals, invoices and AutoInvoice, run asynchronously through the ERP Integration Service with callbacks. SOAP is for objects that only have SOAP services, or legacy integrations. Business events are outbound and event-driven, like invoice approved or PO approved, for near real-time updates downstream. And BICC or BIP extracts push bulk data out to a data lake or other systems. My rule: events for real time, FBDI for bulk inbound, BICC for bulk outbound, REST for transactional lookups."

Tip: State the rule at the end. It's what architects are expected to have.

Likely follow-up

Which would you use for 100,000 AP invoices a day?

FBDI bulk import, orchestrated by OIC — staging the file, calling the import, then polling the ESS job and handling errors — not REST record by record. REST at 100,000 calls a day is slow, hits throttling limits and is harder to recover when something fails halfway.

6How would you design an integration architecture for Oracle Fusion with 50+ systems?IntegrationIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a classic architecture question.

Answer:

"One integration platform, OIC or an enterprise iPaaS like MuleSoft, never point-to-point. Standard patterns: event-driven, scheduled batch, and API-led with system, process and experience APIs. Master data flows with a clear source of truth for each domain, like HCM for workers, Fusion for suppliers and CRM for customers. Central error handling with logging, retries, dead-letter queues, alerts and a reprocessing screen. Security with OAuth, least-privilege integration users and secrets in a vault. Monitoring dashboards with SLAs per integration. CI/CD and regression tests on every quarterly update. And an inventory of every integration with an owner."

Tip: Mention a reprocessing screen for the business. It reduces support tickets to IT.

Likely follow-up

Why avoid point-to-point integrations?

They multiply fast — 50 systems point to point can mean hundreds of connections — and each one is hard to monitor and can break on quarterly updates. An integration platform like OIC gives one place for monitoring, error handling, retries, security and reusable connectors, so support doesn't depend on whoever built each link.

7How do you design a high-volume journal integration from a banking or billing system?IntegrationIn-depth

Why they ask: high-volume journals are a common performance trap.

Answer:

"I summarize at the source where possible, by account and day, because GL isn't a transaction store; detail stays in a subledger or Fusion Accounting Hub. I use FBDI journal import with chunking, say 100,000 lines per file, and parallel imports by ledger. Account combinations are validated before sending, using cross-reference tables. Unique batch IDs prevent double loading. Each batch has control totals, and an automated comparison checks source against GL. Loads are scheduled around close and monitored. And if the business needs detailed accounting rules for that source, I'd use Accounting Hub instead."

Tip: Say "GL isn't a transaction store". It's the key architectural point.

Likely follow-up

Where should transaction detail live if GL is summarized?

In the source system or a subledger like Accounting Hub, with drill-back for audit.

8What is Fusion Accounting Hub (FAH) and when would you use it?Accounting HubIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: to see if you know when to bring in Accounting Hub.

Answer:

"Accounting Hub lets you create subledger accounting from non-Oracle systems, like banking, insurance policy administration, billing or loans, using the same SLA rules engine Fusion uses. You define a subledger application, an event model, sources and accounting rules, load transactions through FBDI or REST, and run Create Accounting to produce journals in GL with drill-down to the source details. It supports more than one GAAP, for example IFRS 17 and US GAAP, through separate accounting methods. I'd use it when the source system can't produce good accounting, or when finance needs to control the rules in one place."

Tip: Give an industry example, like insurance or banking. It anchors the answer.

Likely follow-up

What's the alternative to Accounting Hub?

Importing summarized GL journals directly from the source systems. It's simpler and cheaper, but you lose rule-based control over accounting, consistent account derivation and drill-down from GL to the source transaction. For high-volume billing or insurance systems where auditors need that trail, Accounting Hub is the better design.

9How would you design analytics and data extraction from Fusion Financials?Data ArchitectureIn-depth

Why they ask: analytics design affects production performance.

Answer:

"Fusion Data Intelligence gives a prebuilt warehouse with finance KPIs on Autonomous Data Warehouse, and it can be extended with other data. BICC incremental extracts go to object storage or a data lake for enterprise platforms like Snowflake or Databricks. OTBI and BIP handle operational reporting inside the app. EPM handles planning and management reporting. Governance covers data definitions, like what 'spend' means, reconciliation of the warehouse to GL, and replicating security. I avoid heavy BIP extracts for analytics, because they load production and are hard to maintain."

Tip: End with the BIP warning. It shows experience.

Likely follow-up

When would you skip FDI?

If reporting needs are met by OTBI and Smart View and there's no need for cross-system analytics.

10How do you architect security for a global Finance Cloud deployment?SecurityIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: security design is an architect's responsibility.

Answer:

"Roles are designed around job families, by copying and trimming seeded roles and keeping custom privileges to a minimum. Data security uses BUs, ledgers and data access sets, with segment value security only where it's essential. An SoD rule set in Advanced Access Controls or a GRC tool checks at provisioning. Provisioning is automated from HCM with role mapping rules, and IAM gives SSO through SAML or OIDC with MFA. Privileged access for admins and implementers is time-limited and logged. Audit policies cover sensitive setups, with quarterly access reviews. And integration users get least privilege."

Tip: Mention preventive SoD checks at provisioning. Catching conflicts before access is granted is better than after.

Likely follow-up

How do you handle security for 40 entities?

Design roles by job family — AP clerk, AP supervisor, GL accountant — and give data access by ledger, business unit or data access set, provisioned through role provisioning rules tied to HCM assignments rather than manual requests. Forty entities with manual provisioning quickly turns into SoD and audit findings.

11How do you plan the environment (pod) strategy for an Oracle Cloud program?EnvironmentsBasic

Why they ask: environment planning avoids conflicts and delays.

Answer:

"Typically production, test or UAT, development or CRP, and sometimes extra ones for data migration or training. I plan production-to-test and test-to-test refreshes, and after go-live I refresh test from production regularly for realistic testing. Configuration moves with configuration packages, or manually with checklists, and extensions through proper deployment. Test environments get quarterly updates two weeks early. Sensitive data is masked in non-production. And an environment calendar prevents conflicts between projects."

Tip: Mention data masking after refreshes. It's often forgotten and it's a privacy risk.

Likely follow-up

Why plan pod refreshes carefully?

Refreshes wipe test data and configuration in progress, so they need to fit the project calendar.

12What extension options exist for Oracle Fusion and how do you decide?ExtensionsIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: extension choices affect upgrades for years.

Answer:

"Configuration first: flexfields, lookups, rules, approval workflows and personalization. Then Visual Builder and Redwood extensions for UI on Fusion data through REST. OIC for integrations and process automation. BIP and OTBI for reports. Application Composer and Groovy in some areas. OCI services, like Functions or APEX on Autonomous Database, for side applications. And AI Agent Studio for agentic automation. I decide by business value, upgrade safety using only supported APIs, the skills to maintain it, and security. And I keep an extension inventory that's reviewed every year."

Tip: Mention the yearly inventory review. Unused extensions are hidden cost.

Likely follow-up

What extension would you avoid?

Anything that depends on undocumented internals — like reading application tables directly for transactional logic or scraping pages — because a quarterly update can change them without notice. I'd prefer documented REST APIs, business events, VB Studio, OIC and BI Publisher reports, which Oracle supports through updates.

13How would you architect a phased EBS-to-Fusion migration with coexistence?MigrationIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: most large migrations are phased.

Answer:

"I'd phase by module, Financials first and SCM later, or by entity or region. Coexistence integrations connect the two: EBS SCM receipts and POs to Fusion AP for matching, EBS OM to Fusion AR, and EBS inventory accounting to Fusion GL as summary journals. Master data has one source during coexistence, for example suppliers mastered in Fusion and synced to EBS. A data warehouse supports reporting across both. Coexistence integrations are expensive, so I retire them quickly. And historical data goes to an archive with reporting."

Tip: Say "retire coexistence quickly". It's the cost people underestimate.

Likely follow-up

What's the main coexistence risk?

Data getting out of sync between EBS and Fusion during coexistence — suppliers, customers or the chart of accounts changed in one but not the other. Master data ownership must be clear: one system is the source for each object, changes flow one way, and you reconcile daily.

14How would you integrate an acquired company into the Oracle Fusion Finance platform?M&AIn-depth

Why they ask: M&A readiness is a strategic platform quality.

Answer:

"On day one, I'd consolidate their trial balance into FCCS through a mapping, with no system change. Then assess their processes, systems, chart of accounts, entities and contracts. Onboarding uses the global template: new legal entities, a ledger or balancing values, and BUs, plus localizations. Data migration covers open AP and AR, assets and balances at a month-end. Integrations cover their banks, payroll and operational systems. People and controls cover roles, SoD and SOX scope. And I'd make the platform M&A-ready with a documented template, automation scripts and a repeatable playbook, targeting something like 90 days to onboard."

Tip: Mention the 90-day playbook. It shows you build for repeatability.

Likely follow-up

Why not migrate the acquired company on day one?

It's too risky; consolidating their trial balance first gives reporting while you plan a proper migration.

15How would you support a divestiture (carve-out) of a business unit in Oracle?M&AIn-depth

Why they ask: divestitures are hard if the design didn't plan for them.

Answer:

"First I identify everything belonging to the business: ledgers or balancing values, BUs, suppliers and customers, assets, open transactions and users. Then the options: a transition service agreement, where we run processes for the buyer for a period; a clone of the environment for the buyer; or a data extract for the buyer's system. Data access between us and the divested business is restricted, which is often a legal requirement. Cut-off dates for open items and intercompany settlement are planned. And the lesson for design is to keep separate ledgers or balancing values per entity from the start, which makes carve-outs much easier."

Tip: End with the design lesson. It shows foresight.

Likely follow-up

What's the hardest part of a carve-out?

Separating shared data, like suppliers, customers and contracts, cleanly and on a tight legal deadline.

16Design the bank integration architecture for a multinational using Oracle Fusion.BankingIn-depth

Why they ask: an architect question. They want options, standards, security and monitoring.

Answer:

"There are three ways to connect: host-to-host with each bank, SWIFT through Alliance Lite2 or a service bureau, or a bank connectivity hub. For 15 or more banks I'd lean toward a hub, because each new bank is then a configuration, not a project. I'd standardize on ISO 20022: pain.001 for payments, pain.002 for status, and CAMT.053 and CAMT.052 for statements. Security is PGP encryption and signing, managed SFTP keys, and separation of who releases payments. The flow is: payment process requests create files, the hub sends them to banks, and acknowledgments and statements come back into Cash Management. If treasury needs an in-house bank or netting, that lives in a TMS integrated with Fusion. And I'd monitor every transmission and acknowledgment, with alerts when a file doesn't arrive."

Tip: Recommend one option and say why. Architects are expected to choose.

Likely follow-up

When is a bank hub better than host-to-host?

When you have many banks; one connection to the hub reduces integration and maintenance.

17How would you architect indirect tax for a global Fusion implementation?TaxIn-depth

Why they ask: a senior design question across many countries.

Answer:

"I'd use Fusion Tax natively for VAT and GST countries, where rules-based setup works well, and a partner engine like Vertex, Avalara or Sovos for US sales and use tax, and possibly worldwide if the tax team prefers one engine. E-invoicing mandates, like Italy SDI, Mexico CFDI, India e-invoicing, PEPPOL and EU ViDA, go through Oracle localizations or partners. Tax returns and SAF-T come from the tax reporting ledger or the partner. Master data matters: party tax profiles, registrations and product tax classifications. And governance: the tax team owns the rules, changes go through change control, and every rate change gets a regression test."

Tip: Separate US sales tax from VAT. It shows you understand why most companies use a partner for the US.

Likely follow-up

Why mix native tax and a partner engine?

Native works well for VAT regimes, while US sales tax with thousands of jurisdictions needs a partner engine.

18What non-functional requirements matter for a Finance Cloud design and how do you address them?Non-FunctionalBasic

Why they ask: NFRs are an architect's job.

Answer:

"Performance: volumes like invoices per day, journal lines and AutoInvoice lines, batch windows and close processes, tested with production-like volumes. Availability: Oracle provides the SaaS SLA, so I plan for integration outages rather than the core app. Security and compliance: SOX, GDPR with data residency and personal data in supplier and customer records, and no card data for PCI. Auditability: audit trails and log retention. Scalability for new entities and acquisitions. And maintainability: standard features, documented extensions and regression automation."

Tip: Mention testing with production-like volumes. It's the NFR most often skipped.

Likely follow-up

Which NFR is most often missed?

Performance at month-end volumes, which needs testing with realistic data.

19A Create Accounting job runs for 6 hours at month end. How do you approach it?PerformanceIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a performance troubleshooting question.

Answer:

"First I check volumes and timing: is everything being accounted at month-end? If so, move to daily accounting. Then parallelize by ledger or application and set the processing parameters properly. Then review SLA rules, because complex custom sources, heavy mapping sets or many supporting references slow things down. I check for errors causing reprocessing, and for other jobs, like AutoInvoice or big reports, competing at the same time. If it's still abnormal, I raise an SR with Oracle with diagnostics. And I measure after each change."

Tip: Lead with "move to daily accounting". It usually solves most of the problem.

Likely follow-up

What's the quickest fix?

Run Create Accounting daily, or several times a day, instead of everything at month end. That spreads a month of volume across 30 small runs, so the month-end job only picks up the last day. Then look at partitioning by ledger or source, and check for slow custom sources in SLA.

20How do you architect management reporting vs statutory reporting?ReportingIn-depth

Why they ask: these two needs often conflict.

Answer:

"Statutory and GAAP reporting comes from GL, and FCCS for consolidated, and it's controlled, reconciled and audited. Management reporting often needs different hierarchies, allocations and non-GL data like headcount and volumes, so it goes to EPM, Planning or Profitability and Cost Management, or Fusion Data Intelligence. One chart of accounts with multiple hierarchies supports both. GL stays the single source of truth for actuals, and any warehouse reconciles to it. Narrative Reporting builds board and statutory packs."

Tip: Say "one COA, many hierarchies". It's the design that serves both.

Likely follow-up

Why keep management reporting separate?

It changes often and shouldn't put the controlled statutory process at risk.

21How do Oracle EPM modules fit with Fusion Financials?EPMIn-depth

Why they ask: finance architects need to see the whole Oracle finance stack.

Answer:

"Planning handles budgets and forecasts, and approved budgets can go to GL for budgetary control or reporting. FCCS handles consolidation and close. Account Reconciliation handles balance sheet reconciliations and transaction matching. Narrative Reporting builds management and board packages. Tax Reporting does the income tax provision. Profitability and Cost Management handles allocations and profitability. They connect to Fusion through Data Integration, sending GL balances to EPM with drill-back to Fusion. The key is designing accounts and entities consistently across GL and EPM."

Tip: End with consistent dimensions. It's the architect's real job here.

Likely follow-up

Which EPM module comes first?

Usually Planning or FCCS, depending on whether budgeting or consolidation is the bigger pain.

22How would you architect AI agents in an Oracle Finance landscape responsibly?AIIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: finance leaders want AI, but not at the cost of control.

Answer:

"I'd build agents in Oracle AI Agent Studio, because they run on Fusion data with Fusion security. I'd pick use cases with clear value, like invoice exceptions, collections emails, reconciliation explanations and close insights. Guardrails: the agent acts only within the user's or a defined service role's access, and anything that posts to the ledger or pays money needs human approval. Every prompt, action and decision is logged for audit. No sensitive data goes to an outside model without approval. I'd measure accuracy, time saved and exceptions, and support finance users through the change."

Tip: Say "human approval for postings and payments" early. It's what the controller is listening for.

Likely follow-up

What's your rule for agents taking actions?

Drafts and recommendations by default. An agent can prepare a journal, suggest an invoice coding or flag a duplicate, but anything that changes money or records — posting, payments, supplier bank changes — needs human approval, with the agent's reasoning logged for audit. Autonomy increases only after measured accuracy.

23How do you present architecture options to a CFO and CIO?StakeholdersBasic

Why they ask: architects must communicate with executives.

Answer:

"I start with the business question and the decision needed. Then two or three options, each with build and run cost, risk, time, business impact and future flexibility. Then a clear recommendation and why. One simple diagram, no jargon. And what happens if we don't decide now. For example: 'Separate ledgers per country add two hours to close but make local statutory reporting and divestitures easier. I recommend them only for countries with different fiscal years.'"

Tip: Always give a recommendation. Executives want your view, not just options.

Likely follow-up

How do you keep executives from getting lost in detail?

Lead with the decision, cost and risk, and keep technical detail in an appendix.

24Describe an architecture decision you made that you later regretted. What did you learn?BehavioralBasic

Why they ask: honesty and learning.

Answer (example):

"To save time, I allowed a custom BIP extract to feed a downstream data warehouse. As volumes grew, it slowed production at peak times, and it broke after a quarterly update. We moved it to BICC incremental extracts to object storage. What I learned: use the platform's intended pattern even if it takes longer up front, and think about growth and upgrades in every decision."

Tip: Own it and give a lasting lesson. That's all they want.

Likely follow-up

How did you fix it?

Replaced the custom extract with a supported REST integration and added a rule that new integrations use supported APIs.

25A client wants to add a new segment to the COA two years after go-live. How do you respond?Chart of AccountsIn-depth

Why they ask: a real consulting situation. They want you to push back with options, not just say no.

Answer:

"First I'd ask what reporting is missing, because often it can be done without a new segment: hierarchies, supporting references, descriptive flexfields, projects or an EPM dimension. If a segment really is needed, Fusion doesn't let you just add a segment to a chart that's in use. The options are a new chart and new ledgers with a COA mapping and balance migration, or using a spare future segment if one was designed in. The impact is wide: integrations, SLA rules, reports, EPM, security, cross-validation rules and training. So I'd recommend a business case and doing it at a fiscal year boundary. And the lesson for every new design is to include a future segment."

Tip: Lead with "what reporting is missing?" It shows you solve the need, not the request.

Likely follow-up

What's the usual alternative to a new segment?

Using an existing future segment, supporting references or hierarchies, depending on the reporting need.

26What makes a chart of accounts "future-proof"?Chart of AccountsBasic

Why they ask: COA design is a long-term decision.

Answer:

"Each segment has one clear purpose, no overloaded segments. Value ranges have room to grow, and there's at least one future segment with a default value. Reporting uses hierarchies instead of hard-coded roll-ups. There's an intercompany segment from day one. Values are consistent globally, with controlled local extensions. There's governance for adding values: owners, approval and naming rules. And it lines up with EPM dimensions and consolidation."

Tip: Say "one purpose per segment". It's the rule that prevents most COA problems.

Likely follow-up

Why keep a future segment?

Adding a segment later is very costly, so a spare one gives room for new reporting needs.

27How would you design SLA for complex revenue and cost accounting across multiple GAAPs?Subledger AccountingIn-depth

Why they ask: a senior SLA design question.

Answer:

"I'd start by listing every event class that needs non-standard accounting. Then I'd copy the seeded method once per GAAP, say US GAAP and IFRS, and reuse as much as possible between them, like shared account derivation rules and mapping sets. The second GAAP runs in a secondary ledger with its own method at subledger level. I'd use custom sources sparingly and prefer seeded sources and descriptive flexfields. Supporting references, like contract ID or product, help reconciliation. Every rule gets documented with the business reason and tested with an accounting matrix. And governance is clear: finance owns the rules, IT owns deployment."

Tip: Mention the accounting matrix. It's the test document auditors and controllers want to see.

Likely follow-up

How do you keep multi-GAAP SLA maintainable?

One method per GAAP copied from seeded, with shared mapping sets and clear documentation.

28How would you design an end-to-end intercompany solution?IntercompanyIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: intercompany crosses procurement, finance, tax and treasury. They want the full picture.

Answer:

"I'd start by listing the types of flows: goods through supply chain financial orchestration, services and management fees through Fusion Intercompany, financing loans through treasury, and cost allocations through GL allocations with intercompany balancing. The chart has an intercompany segment and balancing rules by legal entity pair. For each country I decide whether intercompany needs a real invoice or just a journal, because tax and statutory rules often require invoices. Then settlement, usually monthly netting through treasury. Then reconciliation, with Fusion intercompany reconciliation or transaction matching in Account Reconciliation. Elimination happens in FCCS. And transfer pricing policy is agreed with tax."

Tip: Mention invoice vs journal-only by country. It's the decision that tax teams care about.

Likely follow-up

What's the key master data for intercompany?

An intercompany segment in the chart of accounts, plus consistent setup for each pair of entities: intercompany organizations, receivables and payables accounts, balancing rules, and the customer and supplier records if you invoice through AR and AP. Missing setup for one pair is the most common cause of out-of-balance intercompany.

29How would you architect a "continuous close" for a large enterprise?Period CloseIn-depthTop question

Why they ask: a forward-looking architect question.

Answer:

"The idea is to do close work every day, not just at month-end. Subledgers account and post continuously, hourly or daily. Reconciliations are automated, with daily bank reconciliations and transaction matching in Account Reconciliation. Accruals and allocations run on schedules. Close tasks are managed in FCCS or Account Reconciliation with dependencies and dashboards. AI anomaly detection flags unusual journals and balances. Monthly is a soft close, with a hard close quarterly where it makes sense. And I'd track close KPIs and improve them every cycle."

Tip: Open with "close work every day". It explains the concept in one line.

Likely follow-up

What's the first step toward continuous close?

Daily subledger accounting and daily reconciliations — bank, AP, AR and intercompany — so problems are found and fixed during the month. Then month end becomes a final review of accruals, revaluation and reporting rather than a scramble. Many companies cut two to three days just from that change.

30How do you architect data migration for a large Finance program?Data MigrationIn-depth

Why they ask: migration architecture at scale.

Answer:

"I set up a migration framework: extract, stage in a migration database, transform with mapping tables, validate, load with FBDI or REST, and reconcile. Loads and reconciliation scripts are automated so runs are repeatable. Data quality rules feed dashboards, and the business owns cleansing. Mocks are timed for cutover planning, and large loads are tuned. Each reconciliation and sign-off is kept as evidence. And the history strategy is summary balances in Fusion, with detail in an archive or warehouse."

Tip: Mention a staging database. It's what makes migrations repeatable.

Likely follow-up

How many mock conversions would you run?

At least three, with the last as a full dress rehearsal.

31How do you decide what historical data to bring into Fusion?Data ArchitectureBasic

Why they ask: history decisions drive cost and complexity.

Answer:

"I look at legal retention needs, which an archive usually meets better than the new ERP. Then business needs: prior-year comparatives, open items and asset history. Typically that means GL balances for one or two years by period in summary, open AP and AR, full asset records with accumulated depreciation, and open POs. Detailed history goes to an archive or warehouse with search and reporting. Each extra year of detail adds a lot of cost and complexity, so it's a business decision."

Tip: Say "it's a business decision". Architects frame it; the business owns it.

Likely follow-up

What's the usual answer for historical detail?

An archive or data warehouse, not the new ERP.

32How do you handle error management and reprocessing for Finance integrations?IntegrationIn-depth

Why they ask: integration errors are where support time goes.

Answer:
